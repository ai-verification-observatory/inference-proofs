    For policy readers

    Written for analysts, advisers, regulators, and auditors who need to evaluate whether a claim about an AI deployment holds up. No background in cryptography required.

    1. 01Why verifyWhat it means to verify a claim about an AI model, why doing so is difficult in practice, and which slice of the problem this benchmark addresses.
    2. 02What a proof of inference isA cryptographic receipt that lets anyone check that a committed model gave a specific reply to a prompt, without re-running the model or seeing its weights.
    3. 03Three ways to checkRe-run the computation, trust the hardware enclave, or check a mathematical proof: what each approach asks you to trust, and what it costs.
    4. 04A treaty, stage by stageHow proofs of inference would fit into an agreement between two states that refuse to share their model weights, walking through what exists today and what does not.
    5. 05Before you rely on a proofNine practical questions to ask before relying on any inference proof system, and where to find the answers on this site.

    For engineers

    Written for engineers building, running, or auditing these systems: machine learning engineers new to cryptographic proofs, and cryptographers new to LLM inference.

    1. 01The statementThe exact mathematical relation a proof establishes, which copy of the model it covers, and how two published provers state their own guarantees.
    2. 02Inference, for cryptographersWhat a language model actually computes during a forward pass, how production serving works, and why standard floating-point inference is awkward to prove.
    3. 03Proof systems, for ML engineersHow a short random spot-check over polynomials can replace re-running trillions of operations: the core building blocks behind the provers on this ladder.
    4. 04Proving a forward passHow a transformer layer is translated into constraints a verifier can check: matrix multiplications via sumcheck, non-linearities via lookup tables, and weights via polynomial commitments.
    5. 05Design familiesHow different provers represent the computation and commit to the weights, alongside non-cryptographic alternatives: what each design makes cheap, what it makes expensive, and what it has to trust.
    6. 06Where the cost goesWhy proving a reply costs a large multiple of serving it normally, where the GPU time is spent, and how we compute the cost multiple on this benchmark.
    7. 07How we compareOne fixed request per model, six empirical checks, a five-petal glyph, and one cost ratio: the rules of our benchmark and the reasoning behind each choice.

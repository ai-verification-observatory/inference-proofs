    As a machine learning engineer, you already use lightweight spot-checks in place of expensive computations: checking a SHA-256 hash instead of comparing two multi-gigabyte weight files byte by byte, or checking finite-difference gradients at a few coordinates instead of differentiating a loss by hand. Cryptographic proof systems push that idea to its mathematical limit. In most modern proof systems, checking a proof takes a tiny fraction of the compute needed to generate it, the verifier never sees the model weights, and at a 100-bit security level a false result slips through less than once in 2100 attempts.

    Everything the provers on our ladder do is built from four core ideas, and one separate property. Let's work through the first idea by hand and then see how the other three fit on top.

    1. A random spot-check can verify a matrix product

    Suppose someone hands you three n × n matrices A, B, and C, and claims that C = A·B. Multiplying A by B yourself to check their claim takes O(n3) operations. Freivalds' algorithm (new tab) checks the claim in O(n2) operations instead: draw a random vector r, compute B·r, then A·(B·r), and check whether that equals C·r. Because each step multiplies a matrix by a vector, you do three matrix-vector products and never multiply two matrices together.

    If C truly equals A·B, then A·(B·r) and C·r will match for every possible r. If C has even a single wrong entry, the two sides will disagree for at least half of all binary vectors r ∈ {0, 1}n, and for almost every vector drawn from a larger finite field.

    Two rules from this tiny example apply to every proof system on our site:

    • The random challenge must be drawn after the claim is fixed. If a cheating prover knew r ahead of time, it could easily cook up a false matrix C that happens to satisfy A·(B·r) = C·r for that specific vector.
    • Drawing challenges from a larger field drives down the false-pass rate. With random bits, a single check misses a lie at most 1 time in 2; modulo 97, at most 1 time in 97; in a 64-bit prime field, less than 1 time in 1018. Crucially, the arithmetic must take place in a mathematical field rather than wrapping 64-bit integers (uint64), because modulo 264 an error divisible by 263 is missed by every even challenge.

    Of course, Freivalds' algorithm by itself is not yet a proof of inference: the verifier still has to hold all three matrices and do work proportional to their size. The next three ideas remove both requirements.

    2. Polynomials turn a local lie into a global difference

    Take any list of n numbers, such as a row of model weights or a vector of activations. We can view that list as a function on the corners of a boolean hypercube {0, 1}^(log n) and extend it to a unique multilinear extension: a polynomial in log n variables that is linear in each variable separately and can be evaluated at any point over a finite field, not just at {0, 1}.

    Why turn a table of numbers into a polynomial? Because two distinct polynomials of low degree almost never agree! In one variable, two distinct degree-d polynomials can cross at most d times. In multiple variables, the Schwartz-Zippel lemma (new tab) guarantees that two distinct polynomials of total degree d agree on at most a fraction d / |S| of points drawn from a set S. For a multilinear polynomial in log n variables, d = log n. If a cheating prover alters even a single number in its table, the resulting polynomial changes almost everywhere across the field, and evaluating it at a single random point (r_1, ..., r_{log n}) catches the discrepancy with probability at least 1 - (log n) / |S|. Unlike sampling a single row from a database, evaluating the multilinear extension at a random field point mixes every entry of the original table together.

    For the Schwartz-Zippel lemma to hold, arithmetic must be exact, with no floating-point rounding or silent overflow. That is why provers work over a finite field (integers modulo a prime p). Some GPU provers do their bulk arithmetic in a small 31-bit or 64-bit prime field for speed, and then draw their random challenges r from a 128-bit extension field so |S| is huge.

    3. Polynomial commitments check values without the data

    A cryptographic commitment compresses a large array of data into a short digest that is binding (the prover cannot later find a different array with the same digest) and optionally hiding (the digest reveals nothing about the underlying data).

    A polynomial commitment adds one crucial capability: after committing to a polynomial P, the prover can later prove that P(r) = v at any challenge point r by sending a short opening proof that the verifier checks against the commitment digest alone, without ever downloading the polynomial's coefficients. That is how a verifier checks a 400-billion-parameter model on a laptop CPU: the operator commits to the model's weight polynomials once ahead of time, and each inference proof opens that commitment at a few random evaluation points.

    The choice of polynomial commitment scheme shapes a prover's entire performance and trust profile:

    • Elliptic-curve commitments such as KZG (new tab) produce tiny opening proofs, but require a trusted setup ceremony where secret parameters must be generated and then destroyed.
    • Hash-based commitments (such as those built on Reed-Solomon error-correcting codes and the FRI (new tab) low-degree test) require no trusted setup at all and run fast on GPUs, at the cost of much larger proofs (often megabytes rather than kilobytes).

    While the model weights only need to be committed once, the intermediate activations (the witness) change on every prompt and must be committed fresh during every proof, which often accounts for the single largest share of proving time.

    4. Fiat-Shamir hashing replaces live interaction

    In an interactive proof, the prover sends a commitment, the verifier flips coins to pick a random challenge r, and the prover replies. Interactive proofs are inconvenient because they only convince the party who chose the challenges live. The Fiat-Shamir transformation (new tab) makes the protocol non-interactive: instead of waiting for a live verifier to pick r, the prover derives r by hashing the public statement and every message written to the proof transcript so far. Anyone can later verify the resulting file by recomputing those same hashes.

    Fiat-Shamir comes with two important caveats:

    1. Because the prover can compute hashes offline, a cheating prover can try grinding through billions of slightly different commitments until it finds a hash challenge that happens to miss its lie. Parameters must therefore be sized so that even after q hash queries, the probability of finding a lucky collision remains negligible (the random oracle model).
    2. If an engineer forgets to include some part of the public statement or transcript inside the hash input, a cheating prover can modify that omitted value after seeing the challenge and forge a proof. This bug class (“Frozen Heart”) has bitten real systems repeatedly: Trail of Bits disclosed it across several popular libraries in 2022 (new tab), and a 2026 audit (new tab) found and fixed six more instances across four sumcheck-based provers and two STARK provers. Even more subtly, a 2025 paper (new tab) showed that Fiat-Shamir can fail for certain protocols even when every transcript message is hashed, if the attacker is allowed to choose the circuit being proved and tailors it around the hash function.

    Zero knowledge is a separate property

    Nothing in steps 1 through 4 automatically hides the model weights! If a prover sends raw evaluations of its weight polynomial at several points, each evaluation reveals a linear equation over the weights. To make a proof zero-knowledge, the prover must blind its commitments and mask its polynomial evaluations with fresh random noise so that the transcript reveals nothing beyond the fact that the statement holds.

    Keep two distinctions in mind when reading zero-knowledge claims:

    • Succinctness and soundness do not imply zero-knowledge. Many proof systems use “ZK” in their project name while leaving blinding unimplemented or optional for speed.
    • Paper zero-knowledge does not guarantee implementation zero-knowledge. Even a single unblinded opening or side-channel metadata field in the C++ or Rust code can leak weight information. Our “Weights hidden” petal records what each publisher documents about weight hiding at the pinned commit; our harness does not test for subtle cryptographic leaks.

    What an accepted proof actually guarantees

    When a verifier accepts a proof, it means that either the claimed statement holds, or the random challenges happened to land on a point where two unequal polynomials agreed, or one of the underlying cryptographic assumptions was broken. The probability of an unlucky challenge in a single round is the soundness error; accounting for offline hash grinding gives the security level in bits.

    Whenever a system claims a λ-bit security level, ask whether that bound is proved or conjectured. In hash-based STARK and proximity-test provers, choosing how many Merkle branches to query depends on how tightly error-correcting codes behave under correlated hashing. For years, many production systems set their query counts using an optimistic mathematical conjecture that roughly halved proof sizes compared to proven bounds. In late 2025, three papers (one (new tab), two (new tab), three (new tab)) disproved that conjecture in its strongest form. No deployed system was forged, and existing proofs still pass verification, but as the Ethereum Foundation explained (new tab), “what was advertised as 100 bits might actually be 80”.

    Crucially, no black-box benchmark can measure a system's security level by running its verifier. A prover configured for 60 bits of security and a prover configured for 128 bits both accept every honest proof and both reject every tampered input our test suite sends. Distinguishing 60 bits from 128 bits empirically would require executing a 2^60-hash forgery search. Security bits come from mathematical analysis of the protocol and parameters, which is why our checks test input bindings empirically while our glyph reports the publisher's documented security level alongside its basis.

    Quick guide to common proof-system terms

    TermWhat it actually meansWhat it does not imply
    SuccinctThe proof is much smaller and faster to verify than re-running the computationThat generating the proof is fast
    Non-interactiveThe proof is a static file that anyone can verify without talking to the proverThat the system required no trusted setup ceremony
    SNARKSuccinct Non-interactive Argument of KnowledgeThat the proof hides the witness (hiding requires a zk-SNARK)
    STARKTypically a proof system built from hash functions and Reed-Solomon codes with no trusted setupAny specific proof size or proving speed
    TransparentPublic parameters are derived from public randomness with no secret setupThat the proof relies on no unproven cryptographic assumptions
    ArgumentSound against a computationally bounded proverSound against an unbounded adversary (which is an information-theoretic proof)

    Three ways to check

    Re-run the computation, trust the hardware enclave, or check a mathematical proof: what each approach asks you to trust, and what it costs.

    If you want to verify a computation you did not run yourself, you have three basic technical options: re-run the computation on a machine you trust, trust the hardware enclave that ran it, or check a cryptographic proof. Each approach shifts your trust somewhere else, and each has its own failure modes and cost profile. Which one makes sense depends on who is checking whom.

    1. Re-run the computation

    Under recomputation, the checking party (or a third-party server both sides trust) holds a copy of the model weights and repeats the forward pass, either on every request or on a random sample. If the recomputed outputs match the operator's outputs, the claim holds.

    Recomputation is the first mechanism proposed in most AI governance papers, and it is already used internally. Anthropic, which also built one of the provers on our ladder, reports (new tab) that it met its internal 2026 inference-verification goal “through a separate but related project, which signs our inference traffic and recomputes inference on a trusted server”. For the operator serving the model, the extra overhead is near zero; the verifier simply pays the GPU cost of whatever fraction of traffic it chooses to re-run.

    The catch is twofold. First, the arithmetic has to be reproducible, or else the comparison has to allow a numerical tolerance between floating-point runs, and any tolerance opens room for subtle manipulation. Second, and more importantly, a machine outside the operator's normal serving fleet must hold a copy of the model weights. When a company's own security team is checking its own servers, sharing the weights is trivial. Between commercial competitors or rival states, handing over the weights is a non-starter. Proposals for cross-border recomputation try to solve this by placing the recomputation cluster inside a physically guarded room (often inside the operator's own facility) that only emits pass/fail signals. At that point, the weights are entrusted to a physical box rather than the other state, and the entire scheme rests on keeping that box from leaking or being tampered with. As Hausenloy and Li (new tab) observe, “the recomputation cluster sees the records of someone's frontier computations, so it has to be defended like the secrets themselves”.

    2. Trust the hardware enclave

    Modern CPUs and GPUs can run code inside an isolated memory region that the server's operating system and administrator are prevented from reading or modifying, and can sign a cryptographic report identifying the exact binary and weights loaded into that region. The isolated environment is a trusted execution environment (TEE), and the signed report is an attestation.

    Hardware attestation is available on commercial GPUs today and adds very little overhead. A 2025 benchmark (new tab) on an 8-billion-parameter model running on a single GPU found: “The overhead for first-token latency was 9-16%, while the drop in overall throughput under a high-concurrency load was only 2.88%.” The model runs in standard floating-point without modification, and the weights never leave the enclave.

    What you have to trust instead is the chip vendor and the physical security of the chip. An attestation signature is only as trustworthy as the manufacturer's root keys and the hardware's resistance to physical probing. A technical review (new tab) by MIRI's Technical Governance Team points out that anyone with access to the vendor's signing key “can, in principle, produce valid attestation reports for arbitrary measurements without possessing the physical chip”. Academic researchers have also forged enclave attestations (new tab) by attaching an interposer to a server's memory bus using equipment costing “under $1000”. Chip vendors explicitly exclude physical interposer attacks by the machine's owner from their standard threat models. For a customer renting cloud GPUs from a reputable provider, trusting the chip vendor and the data centre's physical security is often completely reasonable. For an international inspectorate checking a state adversary that has unrestricted physical access to its own data centres, trusting a hardware signature alone is much harder to justify.

    3. Check a cryptographic proof

    Under a proof of inference, the operator publishes a commitment to its model weights and generates a mathematical proof for each reply that needs to be checked. Anyone can run the verifier program on that proof, as described in What a proof of inference is.

    Here, the model weights never leave the operator's machines, and verification does not depend on trusting a chip manufacturer or guarding a physical enclosure. Instead, trust moves into mathematical hardness assumptions, the correctness of the verifier code, and whatever process established that the public commitment actually corresponds to the model in question. Anyone given the prompt, the reply, and the proof can verify the result on a commodity CPU, including third parties who had no role in the original run.

    The downside falls squarely on the operator. Generating a proof costs a large multiple of normal inference. Moreover, because current proof systems require deterministic integer or finite-field arithmetic, they prove a quantized variant of the model rather than standard floating-point weights, meaning the operator must serve that quantized variant in production if it wants its live replies to be provable. An operator can also simply refuse to return a proof when challenged. Finally, the software is still experimental: as the Singapore AI Safety Hub (author of the SASH prover) warns (new tab), “bugs in deployed proof systems have let provers prove false statements before”. Before you rely on a proof discusses a recent case where a widely used soundness conjecture had to be revised downward.

    Two other verification tools sit outside this three-way comparison. First, black-box behavioural testing from the outside requires no cooperation from the operator at all: you send prompts to the public API and compare the output distribution against a reference model, which is how researchers caught the undeclared quantizations cited on Why verify. Black-box testing can flag systematic drift across many queries, but says very little about any single reply. Second, human inspectors and embedded auditors can inspect systems on site. As a 2025 governance study (new tab) emphasises, “increased access (e.g., physical inspections of data centers) can often substitute for these technical approaches”.

    Combining multiple approaches

    In practice, these mechanisms are often layered together rather than used in isolation. Anthropic built both a trusted recomputation pipeline and a cryptographic prover, noting (new tab) that “trusted hardware and cryptography are complementary approaches with different guarantees and tradeoffs”. Most architectures proposed for international AI agreements combine several layers at once: tamper-resistant network taps log every prompt and reply at the data centre perimeter, a random sample of requests is drawn after the fact, and the operator answers each sampled request either by re-running it inside a guarded room or by supplying a cryptographic proof. As the Singapore AI Safety Hub points out (new tab), swapping recomputation for a proof inside that sampling loop changes only the last step: “A proof answers the same challenge, and nothing else in the protocol has to change.” Of course, as the same note adds, doing so still requires that “The provider serves the arithmetic the proof constrains.”

    A precedent from nuclear arms control

    Engineers working on nuclear warhead dismantlement ran into the exact same dilemma decades ago. As Glaser, Barak, and Goldston (new tab) framed it, inspectors “will have to gain high confidence in the authenticity of submitted items while learning nothing about them”. The traditional solution in nuclear safeguards is an information barrier: a radiation detector placed inside a shielded box that measures the warhead's classified isotopic signature and illuminates only a green or red light on the outside. Because those electronic barriers “are at risk of tampering and snooping”, Glaser and his colleagues proposed a physical zero-knowledge protocol “designed in such a way that sensitive information is never measured and so does not need to be hidden”.

    Both the parallel and its limits are instructive. An information barrier is the physical analogue of trusted hardware: the secret goes inside a trusted box, and both sides have to trust the box's electronics. A zero-knowledge protocol tries to eliminate the trusted box. Yet the warhead protocol still requires comparing the inspected item against “at least one reference warhead (new tab)”, and its secrecy “remains guaranteed only if the prover follows the protocol”. While the technique has been demonstrated in calculations, simulations, and a laboratory test with non-nuclear objects, it has never been deployed under an active arms control treaty, remaining on the shelf for over a decade.

    A simpler, procedural information barrier does appear in existing treaty law. During a challenge inspection under the Chemical Weapons Convention, the inspected state is allowed to configure the analytical instruments so they report only the “presence or absence (new tab)” of scheduled chemicals rather than full spectra. Because the spectrometer itself still ingests the raw sample, that remains a hardware barrier rather than a cryptographic proof.

    Starting from who has to trust whom

    The quickest way to choose among these three methods is to ask whom both sides are willing to trust. An enterprise customer and a cloud provider can usually agree to trust the GPU vendor. A company and its external auditor can agree on a locked server rack. Two rival states that expect each other to cheat may not agree on any shared hardware or third-party custodian, and that adversarial setting is where cryptographic proofs make the strongest theoretical case.

    The Singapore AI Safety Hub, which works on both hardware enclaves and cryptographic provers, summarises the trade-off candidly:

    TEEs and activation checks are cheap, and for many settings they are the right tool; TEEs are a sensible starting point for a first agreement. What they cost is trust: in a hardware vendor, in a third party, or in a tolerance. A proof moves that trust into cryptographic assumptions and into code that both sides can read.

    Singapore AI Safety Hub, research note, September 2026 (new tab)

    Whether that shift in trust is worth the compute multiple, the requirement to serve quantized integer arithmetic, and the complexity of young cryptographic software is still an open engineering question. Most detailed blueprints for state-to-state agreements today still default to guarded recomputation. A treaty, stage by stage walks through how proofs would fit into such an agreement and shows which pieces exist today.

    Met needs, in full

    A proved bound of at most 2−128 on a fake reply passing the verifier we run.

    What this system has

    The publisher proves, and states that Lean has checked, an upper bound on the chance that a fake reply passes verification: at most q/2124 + 2−101 per proof. Here q is the number of hash queries a cheating prover makes while searching for a forgery. The other term is fixed, while the first grows with q, reaching 2−100 at 224 queries and 2−80 at 244 queries. Each reply has its own proof, generated in batches of 24 (see item 2 below).

    The theorem is about the Lean verifier, a verifier the publisher wrote in Lean and proves things about. We run cp_verify, the publisher's C++ verifier (“Anyone can check the proof on an ordinary CPU in seconds with cp_verify, without the weights.” (new tab)). For cp_verify, the publisher states that it is tested, not proved, and claims no limit.

    Our reading, on one point the grade uses (no single sentence of the publisher's says it): The publisher states the bound “against a committer and a prover who may both cheat” (the committer publishes the weight commitment, and the prover generates proofs). Our rule requires a bound against any arbitrary cheating prover running its own hardware without trusted hardware or an honest third party, so we read this as satisfying the adversary requirement. The publisher notes that nothing is claimed against a quantum computer, which Level 1 does not evaluate.

    Where each item stands today
    1. Today the theorem covers the Lean verifier only.
    2. We generate replies in batches of 24 and verify each reply's proof on its own. The publisher's batch bound covers all 24 proofs only when checked together (24 times the single-proof error across total batch hash queries), with no bound stated for proofs verified one at a time.
    3. The theorem requires “public files that are fixed before the hash function is sampled (a provider that tunes them to the real hash function is outside the statement)”. In other words, the proof treats the hash function as chosen at random after the public files are locked in, so it does not cover files tuned to the actual hash function. No record shows when our files were fixed, and the publisher notes the reader has to take this on trust. The publisher also warns that on files outside its stored examples, the theorem could hold vacuously simply because the verifier rejects every proof (a verifier that rejects everything never accepts a forgery, which tells you nothing about real replies). Outside the grade, in our trial under Formally verified, the Lean verifier accepted 5 of 5 honest proofs on our files.
    4. Today the error terms are q × 2−124 and 2−101.

    From our commit recordthe commitment and the files commit wrote: policy.txt, KL.bin, layout.txt, key.bin, 0.bin

    The commitment ID comes from "a party your user trusts, never from the server being checked or from files sent with the proof". source (new tab)

    "The commitment ID is a hash, in 64 hex digits, of the configuration, the Merkle root and shape of the first weight table, a masked encoding of the weights, and the out-of-domain (OOD) seeds and answers. [...] the ID covers them by design, but the soundness theorem on `verify` is stated at given public files and does not itself say that one ID cannot verify under two sets of them. The ID does not cover the leaf files or the key file's proofs, which `cp_verify` checks on reading." source (new tab)

    From the requestthe prompt, 512 token ids, and the 512-token reply from our record

    "Only the named output positions are covered, and a reply stopped short verifies as a shorter one. [...] A proof names no client or time." source (new tab)

    From the proof1 file: response.proof

    "Some bytes of an honest proof, or of a commitment's files, can be altered and the proof still accepted, for the same true claim; non-malleability is not claimed, and an accept does not show that this exact file came from the prover." source (new tab)

    Decoding rulegreedy, at every reply position

    "the policy under which every checked token must be the integer model's top choice. It is `cp_verify`'s default." The published commitment's acceptance policy is exact match. source (new tab)

    Checked by no call

    "The client must trust what no call checks: the commitment ID, the expected policy and its numbers, the tokenizer and chat template, and `cp_verify` itself." source (new tab)

    Architecturevisible: the architecture and every size

    The declared list, "At least eleven things are visible": each reply, whether a request was served or failed, time, the architecture and every size, settings fitted to the weights, that `cp_commit`'s checks passed, that a batch failed or the service stopped, how much a provider has served and when, who shared a batch and how many, lengths, places in the batch and nonces, masked sums of the weights. "The list says what is visible by design, not that everything else is hidden." In Lean's theorem, one bit per request (served or refused) is revealed, "and it depends on the weights". source (new tab)

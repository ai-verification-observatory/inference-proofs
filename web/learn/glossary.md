### Abstraction of the protocol

A machine-checked soundness proof can target one of three levels, and **an abstraction of the protocol** is the narrowest: it models the cryptographic protocol without this model's computation, or the model without the protocol. The next level, **the protocol**, covers both the protocol and the model's computation. The highest level, **the code this row runs**, covers the actual verifier binary executed on the benchmark. A proof about an abstract specification verifies the protocol (or an abstraction of it), not the compiled code we run.

### Adapter

An adapter is the containerized wrapper the harness uses to invoke a proof system's commands at a pinned commit. It passes only settings documented by the system itself ([what we do](/mandate#what-we-do)).

### AIR

An AIR (algebraic intermediate representation) encodes a computation as a table of numbers, called the trace, constrained by polynomial equations across rows. STARK provers such as [Winterfell](https://github.com/meta/winterfell/blob/2f78ee9bf667a561bdfcdfa68668d0f9b18b8315/README.md?plain=1#L59) take computations in this form.

### Any cheating prover

An arbitrary cheating prover that fully controls its own machine, with no trusted hardware or honest third party assumed. To earn Met on Integrity, a system's soundness bound must hold against any cheating prover.

### Architecture

A model's architecture is its structural layout and dimensions, including its layer count, hidden widths, and attention heads. Because every Level 1 model has open weights, its architecture is already public and no Level 1 petal grades architecture hiding.

### Attestation

An attestation is a signed statement from hardware or an organisation asserting that a specific condition holds. In policy writing, the same word often refers to a formal declaration of compliance with a standard.

### Bar

The bar is the highest-scoring damaged copy of a model that every honest copy still outperforms. To pass the [[Fidelity check]], a system's proved replies must score above the bar. See the [checks page](/checks#fidelity) for the full rule.

### Bound

As a verb, a link in the computation is bound when an accepted proof constrains it, such as tying the weights to their published commitment. A [[claim card]] records what is bound in the publisher's own words, or writes "not stated". As a noun, a bound is a stated upper limit, such as the maximum probability that a false proof is accepted.

### Check

A check is one of the six Level 1 entry tests: [[run check|Run]], [[model check|Model]], [[prompt check|Prompt]], [[reply check|Reply]], [[verify without weights check|Verify without weights]], and [[fidelity check|Fidelity]]. Each check finishes [[passed (check)|passed]], [[failed (check)|failed]], or [[not decided (check)|not decided]]. When a check is not decided, we record why: the system cannot run what the test requires, the check has not run yet, the run did not produce a countable result, or an accepted tampering trial in our adapter has not yet been reproduced with the system's own command.

### Claim card

A claim card records what a system's accepted proofs establish in a given configuration, taken directly from the publisher's documentation at the pinned commit. Every field links to its source line, or says "not stated" where the documentation is silent ([RFC-0002](repo:rfcs/0002-claim-card.md)).

### Commit and proving

Commit and proving is the portion of a [[whole session]]'s GPU time outside [[generation]]: committing to the weights and generating the proofs for all replies. On a system page, Cost shows it alongside generation, each divided by the same serving baseline as Cost vs serving.

### Commitment

A commitment is a short value computed from data, such as a model's weights, that locks those values in for future proofs without revealing them. The verifier always takes the commitment from [the harness's own record](/statement#the-committed-weights), never from files shipped alongside a proof. In policy writing, a commitment usually means a pledge.

### Completeness

Completeness means the verifier accepts proofs generated by an honest prover for true statements, up to at most a tiny stated failure probability. It protects the provider and is the one cryptographic property that testing can directly check. A system page reports completeness as the fraction of honest replies accepted in the [[run check|Run check]], and no glyph petal grades it. In safeguards writing, "complete" instead means a declaration that leaves nothing out.

### Confidentiality

Confidentiality is the glyph petal asking whether checking a proof reveals the model's weights. We grade it on what the publisher states and proves about [[zero knowledge]] ([petal rule](/checks#weights_hidden)). It protects the provider. At Level 1, the [[architecture]] stays visible because every model has open weights.

### Computed by our code from recorded numbers

A grade label (`derived`) indicating that our code computed the figure deterministically from harness-recorded timings and counts, with no hand-entered numbers. Hovering over the figure lists the source records it came from ([RFC-0004](repo:rfcs/0004-metrics-timing-grades.md)).

### Conjectured

A soundness bound is conjectured when it relies on an unproven mathematical assumption rather than a proof. On the [Integrity](/checks#unforgeable) petal, a conjectured bound can reach at most Partly met.

### Control

A control is a deliberately modified input (a negative control) that a working verifier must reject. A control trial counts only when its untouched honest twin is accepted in the same batch. In fidelity testing, controls are reference runs of the model, either honest copies or [damaged copies](/checks#fidelity).

### Cost vs serving

Cost vs serving is the ratio between the GPU time required to generate and prove one reply and the GPU time required to serve the same reply without a proof at full load on the same GPU type. Both sides use GPU-seconds per reply, with the proving side measured over [[whole session]]s (commit, generation, and proving) in v1.0 and over a [[further reply]] in later releases, so GPU hourly price cancels out. See [the cost rule](/checks#multiple) for details.

### Evidence: our runs only, not rerun by anyone else

The `reported` evidence tier: these measurements come from our own [[session]]s and have not yet been rerun by another team. Every Level 1 v1.0 figure carries this tier; a result confirmed by another runner would be labelled accordingly ([RFC-0007](repo:rfcs/0007-evidence-tiers-and-reproduction.md)).

### Exact match

Exact match is a decoding policy requiring every checked reply token to equal the committed model's single top-scoring token. A policy with a [tolerance](/statement#checked-tokens) accepts alternative tokens within a margin.

### Failed

A glyph petal is graded Failed when one of our checks, or our rebuild of the publisher's machine-checked proof, directly contradicts the property. For a check failing, see [[failed (check)]].

### Failed (check)

A check fails when the test produces the opposite of what the rule requires. If a tampered input is accepted inside our adapter, the check flips to failed only after we reproduce the same acceptance using the system's own command outside the adapter.

### Fiat-Shamir

The Fiat-Shamir transform turns an interactive proof into a non-interactive one by deriving each verifier challenge from a cryptographic hash of the statement and transcript so far.

### Fidelity

Fidelity measures how closely the quantized copy a system proves matches the replies of the original released model. The site uses three distinct terms: the [[Fidelity check]] and the Fidelity [petal](/checks#faithful_model) measure [[matched leading tokens]], while the [[Fidelity figure]] reports top-token agreement. Every fidelity figure is shown next to the [[quantization]] the system proves.

### Fidelity check

One of the six [entry checks](/checks#fidelity): the system's proved replies must match more leading tokens of the original model's replies than the [[bar]] does, tested with a paired sign-flip test at p < 0.05.

### Fidelity figure

The Fidelity figure is the percentage of reply positions where the system, when fed the prompt followed by the original model's reply token by token, picks the same next token as the original model (top-token agreement). It does not set any check or petal state, and its label states that it is "computed by the system's own code on the reference reply, not by the proved computation". One check ties it back to the actual proofs: wherever a proved reply still matches the original reply from the start, both models have seen the exact same prefix, so the proof fixes what the proved model outputs there. We require the system's scoring code to pick that exact same token at every such position, and we show the count on the system page.

### Field

A finite field is a finite set of numbers, such as integers modulo a prime, with exact addition, multiplication, and division. Proof systems work over fields because [random checks](/forward-pass#testing-a-product-at-random) rely on the fact that the product of two nonzero numbers is never zero.

### Formally verified

Formally verified is the [glyph petal](/checks#machine_checked) tracking how much of the soundness argument has been checked by a proof assistant ([[machine-checked]]): part of the protocol, an abstraction of it, the protocol with the model, or the actual verifier code we run. Reaching any grade above Not met requires us to rebuild the named proof ourselves. This petal protects the customer.

### FRI

FRI (Fast Reed-Solomon Interactive Oracle Proof of Proximity) is a protocol for proving that committed values lie close to evaluations of a low-degree polynomial. Its authors [designed it](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2017/134/) for proof systems that "require only public randomness for their setup".

### Further reply

The GPU time one more proved reply takes once the [[one-time work]] is done: from the start of the `prove` call to the last reply's proof, generation included, summed over the counted [[session]]s and divided by their replies. Releases after v1.0 print Cost vs serving over it, split into generation and [[proving vs serving|proving]] under Cost on a system page ([the cost rule](/checks#multiple)).

### Generation

Generation is the separate phase of a [[session]] in which the system's own model produces the replies before proving them, for systems that separate generation from proving. Its GPU time is included in Cost vs serving and broken out under Cost on the system page.

### Glyph

The glyph is a five-petal summary showing how close a system comes to an ideal prover. Its [five petals](/checks#glyph) are [[integrity|Integrity]], [[confidentiality|Confidentiality]], [[fidelity|Fidelity]], [[formally verified|Formally verified]], and [[trust assumptions|Trust assumptions]]; an ideal prover would reach Met on all five. The petals are never summed into a single score, and the glyph never changes a system's ranking.

### GPU-second

One GPU allocated to a job for one second. GPU-seconds add across all GPUs bound to a job, so two GPUs running for one second equal two GPU-seconds.

### Greedy decoding

Greedy decoding picks the highest-scoring token at every position. With exact arithmetic and a deterministic tie-breaker, the same model and prompt always produce the exact same reply.

### Halfway point

The arithmetic midpoint between two reference copies' mean scores. A system is shown above a halfway point when our paired sign-flip test gives p < 0.05. The Fidelity petal uses the halfway points between the original rerun, the 8-bit copy, and the 4-bit copy.

### Hash query

A single call to the hash function made by a cheating prover searching for a proof that passes verification. A bound written `q/2^t` grows linearly with the number of hash queries `q`.

### Honest and damaged copies

Reference copies of each model, fixed in the [level](/mandate#level) file before any runs, used to calibrate fidelity. Honest copies are a second run of the original model plus standard 8-bit and 4-bit quantizations. Damaged copies are deliberately degraded (3-bit and 2-bit quantizations, a quarter of layers dropped, or a smaller model); the highest-scoring damaged copy that every honest copy outperforms becomes the [[bar]].

### Harness

The benchmark software (the Python package `iproofs`), which launches each system's adapter inside a container and records timings and traces from outside.

### Inference

Running a trained model forward on an input to generate an output. A language model generates its reply one token at a time.

### Integrity

Integrity is the glyph petal asking whether a provider can get a reply accepted that the committed model did not actually compute. We grade it on the [[soundness]] bound the publisher states for the setup we run ([petal rule](/checks#unforgeable)). It protects the customer and covers soundness only ([[completeness]] is tested by the Run check).

### KL divergence

Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence measures how much one probability distribution differs from another. We report [KL to the original weights](/checks#kl) for diagnostics, though neither check states nor rankings depend on it.

### Ladder

The home page of this site, showing one table per open-weights model (largest first) with one row per system run on that model. The [order rule](/checks#order) ranks systems only within the same model rung, never across models.

### Lean

Lean is a proof assistant that mechanically checks every step of a mathematical proof. A theorem accepted by Lean's kernel is [[machine-checked]].

### Level

A frozen benchmark specification stored in one file, fixing the models, request shape, checks, ranking rule, and constants. Glyph grading rules live outside the level file so they can be refined without starting a [new level](repo:rfcs/0018-level-1-ladder.md#the-glyph).

### Lookup argument

A cryptographic proof that every value in a list belongs to a fixed public table. Inference provers use lookup arguments for [model operations](/forward-pass#lookups) that cannot be expressed with plain additions and multiplications.

### Machine-checked

A proof whose steps have been verified by a proof assistant's kernel, such as Lean's. Everything that still has to be assumed (such as the kernel and its axioms) is the trusted base. The [[formally verified|Formally verified]] petal grades how far a machine-checked soundness proof reaches.

### Merkle tree

A tree of cryptographic hashes built over a list of values. Each leaf hashes one value, each internal node hashes its children, and the root hash at the top serves as a [[commitment]] to the entire list. To reveal one entry, the prover sends the value along with the sibling hashes along its path so the verifier can recompute and match the root. FlashProve's commitment ID hashes, among other fields, "the Merkle root and shape of the first weight table" ([its guide](https://github.com/anthropics/FlashProve/blob/9e2bdd8ff5bf9c62f18263d7dac189b0400b260c/guide/how-it-works.md?plain=1#L32-L37)).

### Met, Mostly met, Partly met

The three positive grades on a glyph petal: Met is the top grade, Mostly met is one step below it, and Partly met is the lowest grade above Not met. Each petal's exact criteria live on the [checks page](/checks#glyph). The site shows these grades in distinct colours without naming colours in text.

### Matched leading tokens

The number of consecutive tokens at the beginning of a reply that match the original model's reply before the first difference occurs. This count is the per-prompt score used by the [[Fidelity check]].

### Missing for Met

The complete list of requirements separating a petal's current grade from Met. On each system page, every item is an open square on its criterion's track, in the cell of the grade it unlocks; opened, the criterion lists each item there in words with who needs to act (the publisher or us), and one click further, where the system stands today.

### Mixture of experts

A model architecture that activates only a subset of its weights for each token. Llama 4 Maverick has about 400 billion total parameters, of which roughly 17 billion are active per token.

### Model check

One of the six entry checks: we modify a single weight after commitment, and no proof produced with the altered weights may be accepted against the original commitment ([rule](/checks#model)).

### Multilinear extension

The unique polynomial, linear in each individual variable, that agrees with a table of values on every corner of the boolean hypercube.

### Multiple

The headline ranking metric: steady-state GPU-seconds per proved reply divided by the serving baseline's GPU-seconds per reply at full load. The ladder labels it Cost vs serving and includes generation time. When a row does not reach a steady state, it has no steady multiple ([cost rule](/checks#multiple)) and reports whole-session GPU time over serving instead.

### Next step

The specific requirements needed to move a petal up one grade, computed directly from its decision table and tagged with who acts (the publisher or us). On system pages, every item needed for Met stands under the grade it unlocks, on each criterion's track (see **Missing for Met**). Undecided petals instead list what is needed to reach a verdict.

### Not decided

A petal grade meaning we cannot yet assign a verdict. It is not a failure and does not sit on the quality scale; it simply tells you what is still waiting to be done.

### Not decided (check)

A check status meaning the check has not run yet, did not produce a countable result, or cannot run on this system. The system page states what is needed to decide it and who needs to act.

### Not met

A petal grade meaning the publisher's own documented properties fall below Partly met. Not met, Not stated, and Failed are the three bottom grades, and none ranks above another.

### Not stated

A petal grade meaning the publisher's documentation at the pinned commit says nothing about the property. Because users cannot rely on an unstated guarantee, silence is graded at the bottom rather than left undecided.

### Number format

Whether the proved copy of a model computes with integers, fixed-point numbers (integers with a fixed scaling factor), or floating-point numbers, along with its average bits per weight.

### One-time work

Work a system does once, whatever the request, that every later reply reuses: committing to the weights, and a setup run a session reuses. From the release after v1.0 it is printed apart in GPU-hours, never divided by the replies, with the number of replies after which it is at most 5 % of the total ([the cost rule](/checks#multiple)).

### Operator

The organisation that serves a model and generates proofs for it, such as an AI lab or API provider. [RFC-0018](repo:rfcs/0018-level-1-ladder.md) also uses the term for the team running this benchmark.

### Our rebuild

Our own execution of a publisher's machine-checked proof using the proof assistant and exact version pinned by the publisher, at the commit we benchmarked. Earning any grade above Not met on Formally verified requires a passing rebuild.

### Passed

A system passes on a model when all six of our [checks](/checks#passed) pass. It shows **Not passed yet** if no check failed but at least one is still [[not decided (check)|not decided]], and **Not passed** if any check failed. It shows **Not ranked** if it passed all checks but lacks a [[position]] for the stated reason.

### Passed (check)

A check passes when its test yields the required result. For tampering checks, passing means the verifier rejected the modified input.

### Pinned commit

The full Git commit hash at which we build, run, and quote a proof system. It is completely separate from a [[commitment]], which cryptographically locks in a model's weights.

### Polynomial commitment

A cryptographic commitment to a polynomial that lets the prover prove the polynomial's value at any verifier-chosen point using a [compact proof](/forward-pass). This allows a verifier to check weight computations without holding the weights.

### Position

A ranked system's standing within its model table, equal to 1 plus the number of systems ranked strictly ahead of it. To receive a [position](/checks#order), a system must pass all six checks and state a numeric security level at its pinned commit for at least one verifier it ships.

### Prompt check

One of the six entry checks: we modify one prompt token, and the verifier must reject the proof ([rule](/checks#prompt)).

### Proof

On a system page, the proof file produced for a reply so the [[verifier]] can check it. A mathematical statement and its formal derivation are called a [[theorem]] instead.

### Proof of inference

A cryptographic proof allowing a verifier to check that a specific reply is what a committed model outputs for a given prompt. In policy writing, "proof" is often used more loosely to mean compelling evidence.

### Proved

A statement is proved when acceptance by a [[soundness|sound]] verifier establishes it under the assumptions listed on the claim card.

### Prover

The software that generates a proof (and, by extension, the party running it). The prover holds the [[witness]].

### Proving vs serving

The proving part of a [[further reply]] (the further reply less its generation) divided by the [[serving baseline]]. It reads what proving costs once the reply's tokens exist, and depends on how many replies one proof covers, which the row states. Printed beside Cost vs serving from the release after v1.0 ([the cost rule](/checks#multiple)).

### Publisher

The organisation that releases a proof system and its documentation, or a model and its weights. We refer to the model as originally released by its publisher as "the original".

### The publisher's words

Verbatim quotes copied from a publisher's documentation at the pinned commit, each linked to its exact source lines. Quotes are always attributed to the publisher and never set a check state on their own.

### Q8_0 and Q4_K_M

Standard 8-bit (`Q8_0`) and mostly 4-bit (`Q4_K_M`) quantized copies of the original model, created with `llama.cpp` at the level's pinned commit. We run them unproved as fidelity yardsticks and show their [[Fidelity figure]] once for each model on the ladder, over its systems' results.

### Quantization

Storing a model's weights or activations in fewer bits to make proving cheaper, at the cost of slight numerical drift that can change outputs. Every system on the ladder proves a [quantized copy](/statement#integer-arithmetic) of the original model, and the [[Fidelity figure]] shows how closely its outputs agree.

### Random oracle

An idealized hash function that returns a fresh, uniformly random output for every distinct input. Most [[Fiat-Shamir]] soundness proofs assume the hash function behaves like a random oracle.

### Range check

A proof that every value in a list fits within a fixed bit width, such as proving each value is below 2^16. Provers commonly implement range checks using a [[lookup argument]] against a table of all allowed values.

### Read as

When a glyph grade depends on our interpretation of a publisher's documents rather than a single explicit sentence, the system page marks it with "our reading" and shows the source at the top of the petal's evidence. **The publisher's words** means a direct statement by the publisher; **our reading** means we combined multiple linked passages because no single sentence states the point; **our test** refers to our benchmark runs; and **our rebuild** refers to running the publisher's proof checker ourselves. Any grade that relies on our reading is capped at Mostly met across all systems, and we never infer claims merely from a construction's name (such as STARK). Before 2026-11-17, the site used the labels "stated", "read as", and "tested by us".

### Recomputation

Verifying a model's output by [running the model again](/deployment) on trusted hardware and comparing the results. It requires access to the weights, enough compute to rerun inference, and either deterministic execution or an agreed tolerance.

### Relying party

Whoever makes a decision based on a verifier's verdict, such as a customer, auditor, regulator, or inspectorate. The relying party runs the [[verifier]] (or trusts someone who does) and decides what an accepted proof establishes.

### Reply check

One of the six entry checks: every proof must cover all reply tokens in the request, and modifying the first, last, or an interior reply token must cause the verifier to reject the proof ([rule](/checks#reply)).

### Request

One prompt paired with its expected reply. At Level 1, every request is 512 prompt tokens followed by a [512-token greedy reply](/checks#passed).

### Residual

What a dishonest prover can still get away with even when its proofs pass verification. FlashProve's [[claim card]] quotes its publisher's documented limits; SASH's card records "not stated".

### Run check

One of the six entry checks: we run the publisher's prover as published at the pinned commit across three cold-start sessions, and every reply must receive an accepted proof ([rule](/checks#run)).

### Sampled

A statistical guarantee obtained by checking a [random subset of outputs](/deployment) rather than every reply. Sampling provides probabilistic detection, not cryptographic certainty about unchecked replies.

### Security level

A bit-length measure of how hard it is for a cheating prover to get a false proof accepted. Because [[soundness error]] typically grows with the attacker's work, it is rarely a fixed probability per attempt. For example, FlashProve states `q/2^124 + 2^-101` after `q` hash queries, so each extra query increases the forger's chance. A stated security level may be proved, [[conjectured]], or unspecified; Level 1 reports it exactly as stated.

### Serving baseline

The GPU-seconds per reply needed to serve the benchmark request without a proof using the model publisher's release at [full load](/checks#multiple).

### Session

One cold-start run of a proof system over a single [[slot]]'s prompts: loading the model, committing to the weights, and proving every reply (120 replies on Llama 4 Maverick, 5 on Llama 3.1 8B). Three complete sessions [count toward a row](/checks#run), one per slot.

### Slot

One of three fixed prompt sets assigned to a model rung. Each slot counts its first complete [[session]], and failed attempts are retried within the same slot.

### SNARK and STARK

Two broad families of cryptographic proof systems. A STARK is typically built from hash functions and requires no trusted setup, while SNARKs often use structured cryptographic assumptions. Neither acronym implies that the proof hides its inputs ([[zero knowledge]]).

### Soundness

The property that a verifier rejects proofs of false statements except with a tiny probability bounded by the [[security level]]. Soundness protects the customer and is graded by the [[integrity|Integrity]] petal based on the publisher's stated bound. It depends on a mathematical proof under explicit assumptions; black-box tests can refute soundness, but never prove it.

### Soundness error

The publisher's stated upper bound on the probability that a verifier accepts a false proof, which underpins [[soundness]]. It is stated for a specific verifier, per proof or per segment, often as a function of the attacker's hash queries `q`. The [Integrity](/checks#unforgeable) petal grades it and lists every gap preventing the bound from covering the exact setup we run, while the full structured reason stays in the board file. A probability of `2^-128` is 1 in 2^128 (roughly 3 in 10^39), much smaller than `2^-100`.

### Statement

The exact proposition established by an accepted proof. For [Level 1 inference provers](/statement), it is: *the model fixed by this commitment, run on this prompt under this decoding rule, produces this reply*.

### Steady state

The portion of a [[session]] after its first release group, once proofs are being emitted at an even pace. A release group is a burst of proofs released together. A row has a [steady state](/checks#multiple) when all three of its counted sessions do.

### Shown above

A system's average is shown above a threshold when our paired sign-flip test across prompts rejects the null at the standard 5 % level (`p < 0.05`). We never report "not shown" without context: "not shown above" means the test did not establish a difference, not that the average is necessarily lower.

### Sumcheck

An interactive protocol that lets a verifier check the sum of a multivariate polynomial over exponentially many points using only a single random evaluation at the end. The verifier does [one short round per variable](/forward-pass).

### Theorem

A mathematical statement accompanied by a proof. On a system page, a theorem is a formal result stated and proved by the publisher, such as an upper bound on the chance that a verifier accepts a forged reply.

### Token

The basic unit of text a language model processes, typically a word or subword represented as an integer ID.

### Tokens per second

The request's 512 reply tokens divided by the GPU-seconds required for one reply on a single GPU, reported as `tokens/s per GPU` with the GPU model named. A row's proved rate over [[whole session]]s and its model's served rate from the [[serving baseline]] at full load are taken on the same GPU type. Dividing the served rate by the proved rate gives [[cost vs serving|Cost vs serving]]. Because raw rates depend on the GPU hardware, they are never used to rank systems ([RFC-0018](repo:rfcs/0018-level-1-ladder.md) CO-8).

### Trust assumptions

The glyph petal asking whether verifying a proof relies on trusting anyone's honesty beyond mathematics, such as a [[trusted setup|setup]] secret or a hardware vendor's signing key ([petal rule](/checks#no_trusted_party)). It protects the customer.

### Trusted execution environment

A trusted execution environment (TEE) is a hardware-enforced enclave inside a processor that isolates code and memory from the machine's host. It signs an [[attestation]] report of what code was loaded, resting on the chip maker's keys and physical integrity of the chip.

### Trusted setup

A one-time ceremony that generates public parameters from secret randomness whose knowledge would allow forging proofs (as in KZG polynomial commitments). The [[trust assumptions|Trust assumptions]] petal earns Met when the publisher explicitly states that soundness rests solely on cryptographic assumptions with no setup secret.

### Verification

Checking a claim against evidence that holds up even when the claimant has an incentive to cheat. Depending on context, the word can also mean checking compliance with a treaty or formally verifying software against a spec. On the ladder, it specifically means running a [[verifier]], whose verdict alone never settles policy compliance.

### Verifier

The software that checks a proof against a commitment and prompt, then accepts or rejects it (taking the reply as an input or reading it from the proof). In policy writing, "verifier" often refers to an inspectorate or auditor, which this site calls the [[relying party]].

### Verify without weights check

One of the six entry checks: the verifier must reach every verdict with no copy of the model weights on disk and no GPU visible ([rule](/checks#no_weight_file)).

### Weights

The learned numerical parameters that define a neural network, about 8 billion in Llama 3.1 8B. For a closed model, the weights are the provider's trade secret.

### Whole session

A measurement scope counting all GPU time in a [[session]] from the start of the `commit` call through the release of the final proof (including commit, generation, and proving, but excluding model loading before commit), divided by the number of replies proved. Cost vs serving, session GPU time, and proved tokens per second all use whole-session scope.

### Witness

All private data the prover uses to construct a proof that the verifier never sees. In a proof of inference, the witness consists of the model's weights and all intermediate layer activations.

### Zero knowledge

The property that a proof reveals nothing beyond the truth of its statement: everything the verifier sees could be simulated without knowing the model weights. Zero knowledge comes in different strengths. Honest-verifier zero knowledge holds only if the verifier follows the protocol; after Fiat-Shamir, it can hold against arbitrary verifiers, typically in the [[random oracle]] model within a bounded number of hash queries. It can be perfect (identical distributions), statistical (indistinguishable up to a tiny distance), or computational (hard to distinguish for bounded attackers). Public parts of the statement, such as model dimensions, prompt, and reply, remain visible. Zero knowledge protects the provider, and the [[confidentiality|Confidentiality]] petal reports the exact form the publisher states, requiring coverage of both the actual programs we run and our batch size to reach Met.
